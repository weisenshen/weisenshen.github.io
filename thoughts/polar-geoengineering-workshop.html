Notes from a Polar Geophysicist at a Geoengineering Workshop

Written at Midway while waiting for my flight home.

I spent the past 24 hours at a small workshop on climate systems engineering at the University of Chicago. I came as a polar geophysicist. Here is the take-home message.

The science is genuinely contested. Geoengineering - using technology to deliberately cool the planet or slow down ice-sheet melting - is not fringe. Stratospheric aerosol injection, sea curtains around glaciers, and basal water drainage from ice sheets are being seriously evaluated by serious scientists who seriously disagree with each other. The latter two were not part of this workshop, but they are part of the broader conversation. A 42-author paper published last September declared all polar geoengineering concepts infeasible and dangerous. The rebuttal came within weeks. Both sides have real arguments. I left without a strong opinion, which felt appropriate and safe, at least for now.

Read the critical assessment and the commentary/rebuttal.

There is also an institutional asymmetry. The U.S. federal government published a research mandate on solar radiation modification in 2022, so it was not surprising to see people from NOAA in the room. No U.S. federal agency, as far as I know, has an equivalent research mandate on polar engineering concepts. That difference matters. A topic can be scientifically contested and still have a bureaucratic home. Another can be physically urgent and still have no obvious agency owner.

Our work matters here more than I expected. Nearly every glacial intervention concept depends on physical boundary conditions: bedrock geology, geothermal heat flux, and subglacial hydrology. Those are exactly the kinds of environments field surveys like mine characterize and assess. At this point, the feasibility question is underdetermined not because the engineering is obviously impossible, but because we have not adequately mapped the environment. That is a solvable problem, whether or not one pursues geoengineering.

UChicago is building something interesting. The Institute for Climate and Sustainable Growth - built around energy policy, battery technology, and climate engineering - raised $125 million in its first 125 days. It is less a science institute than a strategic bet: that the University of Chicago's economics tradition, combined with acquired scientific talent, can occupy a distinctive position in the climate transition conversation. Whether that bet pays off scientifically is an open question. As institutional design, it is instructive.

I made a few useful connections. Time to board.

The author is an associate professor of geosciences at Stony Brook University.
